# Offer

Owns `strategy/offer.md` and the claims `offer-relieves-the-blocker`,
`offer-is-better-than-today`, `they-will-switch`, `what-we-sell`.

Read [../DISCIPLINE.md](../DISCIPLINE.md) before writing anything.

## Fit is a claim, not a diagram

Fit means something narrow and checkable: for each obstacle the customer
actually named, the offer does something specific about it, and that something
beats their current workaround by enough to justify moving.

Build the table. Be ruthless about the third column, which is where the honesty
lives.

| What gets in their way | What the offer does about it | Better than today because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| | | |

Rules:

- Left column entries must come from a transcript. No obstacles invented to
  justify a feature the founder already wants to build.
- A row with a weak third column is not a feature, it is a distraction. Cut it
  and say you are cutting it.
- Eight rows means you are describing a platform to people who asked for a fix.
  Ask which single row would make them switch on its own. That row is the
  product; the rest is roadmap.

## The claim founders skip

`they-will-switch` is where most businesses actually die, and it is almost never
discussed, because fit feels like the finish line. Switching has costs the
founder does not pay: retraining, migration, the political risk of having
championed something new, the sunk cost of what they bought last year.

Ask directly: **what would they have to stop doing, and who would notice?**

Then ask what the gain is worth in the same units. If the gain does not
obviously clear the cost, the offer is a vitamin, and you should say the word
out loud.

## What you sell, exactly

Three questions that quietly determine the shape of the whole business:

1. **What is the unit?** What increments when they pay more? A seat, a
   transaction, an outcome, a month of your attention. This choice decides what
   your customers will optimise against you.
2. **When does value arrive relative to payment?** Value after payment is a
   trust problem, solved with proof or a smaller first step. Value long after
   payment is a churn problem, and no amount of onboarding fixes it.
3. **What is the smallest thing you could charge for that still relieves the
   blocker?** That is the wedge. Insist on finding it; founders resist because
   the wedge feels too small to be a business, which is precisely why it is
   available.

## Watch for these

- **Fit to an unranked problem.** The offer fits perfectly, and the problem it
  fits sits ninth on the buyer's list. Check the rank from the customer stage.
- **Better on a dimension nobody buys on.** Faster, cleaner and more elegant are
  not purchase reasons unless someone is measured on them.
- **The offer that requires them to change first.** If the value depends on the
  customer adopting a new discipline, you are selling the discipline, and that
  is a much harder sale than the founder thinks.
- **Bundling to hide weakness.** Six mediocre things in a package is how a
  founder avoids finding out which one nobody wants.

## Writing the document

Update the claim statements to be specific enough to be wrong.
`offer-relieves-the-blocker` should name one obstacle and one mechanism, not a
category of benefit.

Mark `they-will-switch` as `indicated` only when someone changed their
behaviour: a deposit, a signed pilot, a calendar invite they actually kept.
Enthusiasm on a call is not behaviour.

## Handoff

Move to `flawline-model` when `what-we-sell` is a sentence a buyer would repeat
back accurately. The model stage prices what this stage defined; an undefined
unit of sale cannot be priced, only guessed at.
