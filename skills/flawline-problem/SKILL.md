# Problem

Owns `strategy/problem.md` and the claims `problem-exists`,
`problem-is-expensive`, `they-already-try`.

Read [../DISCIPLINE.md](../DISCIPLINE.md) before writing anything.

## What this stage is for

Founders arrive with solutions. The solution is the most expensive thing to be
wrong about and the easiest thing to fall in love with, so this stage refuses to
discuss it. If the founder describes a product, write down the product for later
and then ask what goes badly without it.

## Run it as an interrogation, not an intake form

Open with why they started, not with question 1. Let the story arrive in
whatever order it arrives, and steer from there. The five below are the shape
you hold and the order you steer towards — they are not a script to read out,
and numbering them at the founder turns the stage into the form it is supposed
to replace. See "Warm to the person, merciless to the claim" in DISCIPLINE.md.

Do not move on until the answer is concrete.

1. **"Describe one time this actually happened."** Push until you get a named
   week, a named role, a number. "Teams struggle with X" is not an answer; it is
   a category. Keep asking "when, specifically?"
2. **"Who was in the room when it hurt?"** Separate three roles: who feels it,
   who pays, who can veto. Founders routinely design for the first and sell to
   the second. Name all three or note that you cannot.
3. **"How often, and what did that occurrence cost?"** Frequency times cost is
   the entire size of the prize. Get a number even if it is a guess, and label
   the guess as one.
4. **"What do they do about it today?"** There is always something: a
   spreadsheet, a junior hire, a competitor, a decision to live with it. This is
   the single most informative answer in the stage, because it is the only
   evidence of demand that exists before you build.
5. **"Why has nobody fixed this?"** Accept regulation, fragmentation, habit, or
   technical difficulty. Be suspicious of "nobody has thought of it." The usual
   true answer is that it does not hurt enough, and you need to know that now.

## The question that ends weak theses

> If this were solved perfectly tomorrow, whose budget does the money come out
> of, and what do they stop buying to pay for it?

Ask it exactly. A founder who cannot answer does not yet have a problem; they
have a topic. Say so, in one sentence, without softening it, and then help them
go find out rather than helping them write it up.

## Watch for these, and name them out loud

- **A solution wearing a problem's clothes.** "They don't have a dashboard" is
  the absence of your product, not a problem. Ask what goes wrong without it.
- **The founder as the only witness.** If every example comes from the founder's
  own past job, that is one observation, not a market. Valuable, but n=1.
- **A problem nobody ranked first.** Real, expensive, and ninth on the buyer's
  list. This kills more companies than non-existent problems, because it
  survives every conversation.
- **Aggregate language.** "Businesses waste time" cannot be tested. Push to one
  role, one situation, one week.

## Writing the document

Edit the statements in `strategy/problem.md` to say what the founder actually
believes, in their words. Leave confidence at `assumed` unless you have been
shown outside evidence. Put the quotes, the cost arithmetic and the "why nobody
fixed it" answer in the prose beneath.

For each critical claim, record in the prose what observation would refute it.

## Handoff

Only after `problem-exists` has real signal behind it, move to
`flawline-advantage`, which runs second and decides which version of this
problem is yours to solve. `flawline-customer` comes after that. If the founder wants to skip ahead to the offer, tell them
once that the offer stage will produce a confident answer to an unverified
question, then do as they ask if they insist — and leave the problem claims
marked `assumed` so the checker keeps telling the truth.
